## 7. Methodological Comparison

The literature reveals four distinct methodological paradigms applied to wildfire danger, spread, and ignition modeling.

### Table 7.1: Methodological Comparison Matrix
| Methodological Family | Representative Approaches | Data & Computational Requirements | Key Strengths & Capabilities | Documented Limitations & Failure Modes | Applicability to TERRA-FIRE |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Physical & Semi-Empirical Combustion Models** | Rothermel (1972), FARSITE, Prometheus, Canadian FWI (Van Wagner, 1987). | 1D fuel bed parameters (fuel load, surface-area-to-volume ratio, moisture of extinction), uniform wind vectors. | Grounded in thermodynamics and fluid mechanics; provides deterministic rate of spread (ROS) and flame length. | Requires calibration of dozens of physical parameters rarely available across tropical/montane Mexico; assumes spatial homogeneity. | **Secondary:** Principles inform our understanding of fuel desiccation thresholds, but the complete physical equations are too rigid for national 10m scaling. |
| **2. Classical Remote Sensing Thresholding** | Fixed index thresholding (NDVI < 0.3, NDMI < 0.1), NASA FIRMS thermal anomaly detection (Giglio et al., 2016). | Cloud-free multispectral optical or thermal infrared imagery; basic band arithmetic. | Extremely low computational complexity; easily automated; near-real-time global coverage via geostationary/polar satellites. | High false alarm rates during seasonal phenological changes; thermal sensors are strictly reactive (3–12h lag post-ignition). | **Baseline / Ground Truth:** Used to generate historical training labels (FIRMS thermal anomalies) and raw biophysical features (NDMI). |
| **3. Deep Learning & Computer Vision** | Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), U-Net segmentation, ConvLSTM spatiotemporal networks (Barmpoutis et al., 2020). | Dense, multi-temporal raster cubes; high-performance GPU clusters; large quantities of spatially contiguous labels. | Automatically learns spatial hierarchical features and complex spatial convolutions without manual feature engineering. | Extreme computational burden; black-box opacity prevents tactical trust; highly sensitive to cloud artifacts; poor performance on non-gridded tabular data. | **Hold / Assess:** Inefficient for sparse tabular fire data across national scale; requires massive compute unavailable to municipal rural fire agencies. |
| **4. Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT)** | **XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016)**, LightGBM, CatBoost with TreeSHAP explainability (Jain et al., 2020). | Tabular spatiotemporal matrices (flattened vectors of spectral, terrain, and climate variables); moderate CPU/GPU. | **Highest benchmarked accuracy on heterogeneous tabular spatial data; natively handles missing values; robust to non-linearities; exact TreeSHAP explanations.** | Requires structured tabular feature engineering; requires spatial-block cross-validation to prevent spatial autocorrelation leakage. | **Primary Choice (Adopt):** Forms the core predictive engine of TERRA-FIRE due to its balance of mathematical precision, speed, and interpretability. |

### 7.2. Synthesis of Methodological Trade-Offs
As demonstrated by Jain et al. (2020), while deep learning architectures dominate unstructured image classification, **gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost) consistently outperform deep neural networks on heterogeneous tabular geospatial data**. Wildfire ignition is governed by localized, point-wise interactions between vegetation water content, topographic slope, and atmospheric evaporative demand. Flattening these multi-source rasters into an optimized columnar Parquet feature store and applying XGBoost achieves faster convergence, superior handling of severe class imbalance (via scale-pos-weight parameterization), and instantaneous feature attribution through TreeSHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017).

## 8. Scientific Findings Comparison

An inter-study synthesis of empirical findings identifies strong scientific consensus alongside critical methodological divergences.

### 8.1. Consistent Findings Across Literature
1. **Dominance of Atmospheric Evaporative Demand (VPD):** Studies by Seager et al. (2015), Abatzoglou & Williams (2016), and Jain et al. (2020) uniformly conclude that Vapor Pressure Deficit is the single most predictive atmospheric variable for wildfire ignition and extreme fire spread. When daytime VPD exceeds **2.0 to 2.5 kPa**, fine dead fuels equilibrate to ambient dryness within two hours, creating explosive ignition vulnerability.
2. **Superiority of SWIR-Based Moisture Indices Over NDVI:** Investigations by Yebra et al. (2013) and Chuvieco et al. (2020) prove that traditional Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) tracks chlorophyll concentration and leaf area index, which often remain green and elevated even when foliage is severely dehydrated. In contrast, the Normalized Difference Moisture Index (NDMI, utilizing Sentinel-2 Band 8 NIR and Band 11 SWIR) directly targets the $1.6\,\mu\text{m}$ liquid water absorption band, achieving an $R^2 > 0.75$ correlation with field-sampled Live Fuel Moisture Content (LFMC).
3. **Severe Operational Latency of Orbital Thermal Anomalies:** Evaluations of the MODIS/VIIRS active fire pipeline (Giglio et al., 2016; Barmpoutis et al., 2020) confirm that spaceborne thermal alerts exhibit an unavoidable observation latency of 3 to 12 hours. Consequently, systems based on thermal anomalies cannot prevent fires or provide pre-ignition warnings.
4. **Inflation of Accuracy Metrics from Spatial Autocorrelation:** Roberts et al. (2017) and Jain et al. (2020) demonstrate that evaluating spatial wildfire models using conventional random k-fold cross-validation inflates ROC-AUC scores by 20% to 40% due to "spatial data leakage" (training and testing on geographically adjacent pixels). Valid models must employ spatial-block cross-validation.

### 8.2. Inconsistent Findings and Contradictory Evidence
- **Optical vs. Microwave Radar for Fuel Moisture:** While Yebra et al. (2013) demonstrate the high precision of optical SWIR indices for LFMC under clear skies, they note complete failure under cloud cover. Conversely, Chuvieco et al. (2020) review Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR C-band backscatter) as a cloud-penetrating alternative; however, radar backscatter is confounded by surface roughness, soil moisture, and complex canopy geometry, making generalized calibration across mixed montane ecosystems highly unstable without extensive ground-truth tuning.
- **Complexity vs. Performance in Climate Downscaling:** Muñoz-Sabater et al. (2021) observe that simple adiabatic lapse rate topographic correction ($0.0065^\circ\text{C}/\text{m}$) accounts for over 70% of temperature variation in complex terrain, whereas sophisticated dynamic regional climate models (WRF) yield only marginal improvements in surface relative humidity while demanding 1,000x more compute.
